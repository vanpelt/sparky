The inverter is the translator

Modules make DC at whatever voltage the string happens to sit at. The inverter turns it into 240 V, 60 Hz AC locked to the grid's own phase — it follows the utility's waveform rather than setting its own. Oversize the array against the inverter and the midday peak gets clipped flat; that is usually a deliberate trade, not a fault.

The meter runs both ways

Solar power takes the shortest path: it feeds your loads first, at the panel, before anything reaches the meter. Only the leftover backfeeds through a bidirectional meter into the utility's transformer, where your neighbors consume it. The meter counts each direction in its own register.

Exported kWh are worth less than the ones you keep

Under older net metering, an exported kWh cancelled an imported one, 1:1. Newer net-billing rules (California's NEM 3.0, for instance) pay an avoided-cost rate that can be a fraction of retail — so the value of the system shifts toward self-consumption, which is what the battery buys you.

In a blackout, the panels shut down too

A grid-tied inverter must stop within milliseconds when the line goes dead — anti-islanding, required by UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 — so it can't energize a line a crew believes is de-energized. Backup power needs a battery plus an automatic transfer switch that islands the house first.
